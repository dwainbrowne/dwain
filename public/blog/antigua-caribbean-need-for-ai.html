Business in the age of AI · October 21, 2026

Antigua, the Caribbean and the need for AI.

In October 2026 I set out to run a free AI seminar for business owners in Antigua. I emailed 29 organizations to make it happen, and 19 of them never answered. That silence taught me more than any slide deck could. It shows exactly where AI can help Caribbean businesses, and how much room there is to grow.

The business case

Here's the short version. I wanted to bring practical software and AI know-how from North America to the islands, for free. To do that I needed a room, and I hoped to find a few sponsors and partners. So between late September and October 21, I emailed 23 possible sponsors and partners, plus 6 venues.

The results:

  • 23 sponsors and partners emailed. 17 never answered. 5 gave me a human reply, and all 5 said no this time. 1 sent an automated "under review" message.
  • 6 venues emailed. 4 replied, 2 never answered, and only 1 gave me terms I could book.
  • Across all 29 organizations: 9 human replies, 1 auto-acknowledgement, 19 with no answer at all.
  • Sponsors secured: zero.

I'm sharing this because the numbers describe a pattern I see everywhere, and the pattern is fixable. When most inquiries get no reply, not even a "no", that's a process problem. Process problems are exactly what AI and good software are good at.

The business case for AI in the Caribbean comes down to this. A business that answers fast, books cleanly and takes payment safely wins customers that a slower competitor never even knew it lost. Those customers don't send a complaint. They just go somewhere else.

What the outreach looked like

I'll walk through it in aggregate. The pattern is what matters here.

Sponsors and partners

On October 1 we invited 18 organizations: banks, credit unions, accounting firms and professional bodies. One more address bounced. Some got a follow-up on October 5, more follow-ups went out on October 19, and partner cards went out on October 21. On October 19 I also reached out to 5 more, telecoms, a utility and insurers, and followed up on October 21.

Here's how it landed. 13 of the first 18 never replied across three weeks and several follow-ups. 4 of the second 5 never replied either. The 5 who did reply were gracious about it. All 5 said not this time, and 2 said they'd like to work together on a future event.

I'll take every one of those answers. A clear "not this time" lets me plan. Silence doesn't.

Venues

I emailed 6 venues, starting September 29. Two never answered at all, even after a nudge. Two said no. One sent rates and showed interest, but never got to firm terms. One gave me terms I could book.

That one was the Trade Winds Hotel. They sent quotes on September 29, October 6 and October 8, and confirmed on October 21. The seminar is booked in their Azure Conference Hall: Tuesday, October 27, 2026, 7 to 9 PM, 60 theater-style seats, EC$1,000 all-in. They were the most responsive venue I dealt with, and I'm grateful.

I'll add one thing from my own experience. For some of the bigger venues, I struggled to even find a website with a way to ask about event space. If a customer can't find you, they can't book you.

The demand is real

Here's the part that keeps me hopeful. We've had more than 140 applications for 60 seats. As of October 19, new applications were coming in at 10 to 15 a day.

So the appetite is there. Business owners in Antigua want to learn how to use AI and modern software. The gap is between what people want and how fast the systems around them can respond.

A hotel in Antigua competes with New York

This is the mindset shift I care about most.

A hotel in Antigua isn't really competing with the hotel next door. It's competing with the Hilton in New York or Paris. Your guests have booked rooms all over the world. They compare your booking flow, your response time and how much they trust your payment page against the best they've ever seen.

The same goes for an accounting firm, an insurer, a credit union or a tour operator. Your customer has an inbox full of businesses that reply in minutes. When you reply in a week, or never, you're measured against them.

"This is just the way it is" is the mindset to retire. It's how it is today. It doesn't have to be how it is next year.

The PDF card form

Here's a concrete example, and it's a very common one.

A payment step I ran into while booking event space was an editable PDF credit-card authorization form, sent by email, with no secure payment link.

I want to be fair here. It's a very common pattern. But think about it from the customer's side. You type your card number into a PDF, attach it to an email, and send it off. You have no idea who opens it, where it's stored or how long it sits in an inbox.

A secure payment link fixes that. The customer pays on a proper checkout page, the business gets paid faster, and nobody's card number sits in an email thread. That's a small change with a big effect on trust.

Feature parity and beyond

This is why I'm excited about AI for Caribbean businesses.

For a long time, the best tools were built for big companies in big markets. A small firm in St. John's had to make do with email, spreadsheets and PDFs, because the alternatives were expensive or built for someone else.

AI changes that. A five-person business can now have the same kind of tools a North American firm uses:

  • An inbox that drafts replies so no inquiry sits unanswered.
  • Lead follow-up that flags who's ready to buy and reminds you to call them.
  • Quotes and invoices that go out the same day.
  • Payment links instead of card forms in email.
  • Simple automations that handle the admin so the team can focus on customers.

Small teams can do more with less. And the businesses that move first can leapfrog, because they're not carrying years of old systems. They can skip straight to the way it should work. If you want the technical side of that, I wrote about building a business on Cloudflare.
